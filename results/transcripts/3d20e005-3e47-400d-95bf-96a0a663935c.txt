[USER]: You are an epistemic investigation team. Your task is NOT to produce a dietary recommendation — it is to produce a calibrated epistemic map of the eggs-and-cardiovascular-disease evidence base.

BACKGROUND:
The question of whether egg consumption increases cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk has been studied for 50+ years. Dietary guidelines have reversed multiple times. Current expert opinion is split between 'eggs are neutral/beneficial' (American Heart Association revised 2020) and 'eggs increase LDL and CVD risk' (several prospective cohort analyses).

KEY EVIDENCE STREAMS TO CONSIDER:
1. Large prospective cohort studies (Harvard Nurses' Health Study, Health Professionals Follow-up Study) — generally show little association between 1 egg/day and CVD events in healthy populations
2. PREDIMED trial and similar RCTs — show Mediterranean diet (which includes eggs) is cardioprotective, but eggs are not isolated as the variable
3. Chinese Kadoorie Biobank (N=500k) — showed inverse association between egg consumption and CVD in Chinese population
4. NHANES/MESA cohort analyses (Zhong et al., JAMA 2019) — found positive association between dietary cholesterol/eggs and CVD risk and mortality
5. Mechanistic evidence: dietary cholesterol raises LDL-C in some individuals ('hyper-responders'), but effect is highly variable
6. Confounding: egg consumers in Western cohorts often have other unhealthy dietary patterns (high processed meat, low vegetable intake)
7. Industry funding patterns in egg nutrition research and their documented association with null results

Produce a structured epistemic map with the following components:
A. The 2-3 key CRUXES — specific methodological or factual questions where resolution would most clarify the evidence
B. EVIDENCE QUALITY assessment for each stream — including known confounders and methodological weaknesses
C. CORRELATED EVIDENCE — identify streams that appear independent but share methodological assumptions (e.g., dietary recall, cohort selection)
D. CALIBRATED ASSESSMENT — for a healthy adult eating 1 egg/day, what is the probability that this meaningfully increases CVD risk (e.g., >5% relative increase)? Give a probability range with conditions (e.g., '15-30% probability of a meaningful increase, wide CIs, conditional on not having familial hypercholesterolemia')
E. WHAT REMAINS UNRESOLVED — what would a decisive study need to look like?

### A. Key Cruxes
1. **The Hyper-Responder Threshold:** Does the genetic/metabolic variability in cholesterol absorption (e.g., APOE4 status, cholesterol absorption efficiency) create a bimodal risk distribution that is currently being washed out by population-level averaging?
2. **The Substitution Effect:** Is the risk associated with eggs actually a risk of *what is not being eaten* when eggs are consumed (e.g., fiber-rich plant proteins), or what is being eaten *alongside* them (e.g., bacon/refined grains)?

### B. Evidence Quality Assessment
*   **Prospective Cohorts (Harvard/Nurses):** *Moderate Quality.* High N, but relies on Food Frequency Questionnaires (FFQs), which are notoriously prone to recall bias and social desirability bias.
*   **PREDIMED/RCTs:** *High Quality (for diet patterns), Low Quality (for eggs).* Excellent control for overall diet, but eggs are a component, not an isolated variable.
*   **Chinese Kadoorie Biobank:** *Moderate Quality.* Large scale, but reflects a population with a vastly different baseline diet (lower saturated fat intake) than Western cohorts, limiting generalizability.
*   **NHANES/MESA (Zhong et al.):** *Moderate Quality.* Sophisticated statistical adjustment, but still vulnerable to residual confounding—specifically, the "healthy user bias" where egg consumption correlates with socioeconomic status and health-seeking behaviors.
*   **Mechanistic Evidence:** *High Quality (for physiology), Low Quality (for clinical outcomes).* We know cholesterol raises LDL in some, but we lack a clear link between that transient LDL spike and hard clinical endpoints (CVD events) in the context of whole-egg consumption.

### C. Correlated Evidence
The majority of observational streams (1, 3, and 4) share a fatal methodological dependency: **Self-reported dietary intake.** Because they all rely on FFQs or 24-hour recalls, they share the same systematic error: the inability to accurately capture the *preparation method* (fried vs. poached) and the *accompanying foods*, which are likely the primary drivers of CVD risk, not the egg itself.

### D. Calibrated Assessment
For a healthy adult (no familial hypercholesterolemia, no pre-existing CVD) consuming 1 egg/day:
*   **Probability of a >5% relative increase in CVD risk:** **5–15%.**
*   **Conditions:** This probability is highly conditional on the *total dietary pattern*. If the egg replaces a high-glycemic breakfast, the risk is likely neutral or negative (beneficial). If the egg is added to a high-saturated-fat/high-refined-carb diet, the risk shifts toward the higher end of the range. The wide confidence interval reflects the "hyper-responder" uncertainty.

### E. What Remains Unresolved
A decisive study would require:
1. **Metabolic Ward Design:** A long-term (12+ month) controlled feeding trial where participants are randomized to egg vs. non-egg groups while keeping all other macronutrients and fiber intake identical.
2. **Genomic Stratification:** Pre-screening for cholesterol absorption markers (e.g., ABCG5/G8 polymorphisms) to determine if the "egg-CVD" link is a genetic interaction rather than a universal dietary rule.
3. **Hard Endpoint Focus:** Moving away from LDL-C as a surrogate marker and focusing on coronary artery calcium (CAC) progression or major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE).
