BACKGROUND:
The question of whether egg consumption increases cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk has been studied for 50+ years. Dietary guidelines have reversed multiple times. Current expert opinion is split between 'eggs are neutral/beneficial' (American Heart Association revised 2020) and 'eggs increase LDL and CVD risk' (several prospective cohort analyses).

KEY EVIDENCE STREAMS TO CONSIDER:
1. Large prospective cohort studies (Harvard Nurses' Health Study, Health Professionals Follow-up Study) — generally show little association between 1 egg/day and CVD events in healthy populations
2. PREDIMED trial and similar RCTs — show Mediterranean diet (which includes eggs) is cardioprotective, but eggs are not isolated as the variable
3. Chinese Kadoorie Biobank (N=500k) — showed inverse association between egg consumption and CVD in Chinese population
4. NHANES/MESA cohort analyses (Zhong et al., JAMA 2019) — found positive association between dietary cholesterol/eggs and CVD risk and mortality
5. Mechanistic evidence: dietary cholesterol raises LDL-C in some individuals ('hyper-responders'), but effect is highly variable
6. Confounding: egg consumers in Western cohorts often have other unhealthy dietary patterns (high processed meat, low vegetable intake)
7. Industry funding patterns in egg nutrition research and their documented association with null results

Produce a structured epistemic map with the following components:
A. The 2-3 key CRUXES — specific methodological or factual questions where resolution would most clarify the evidence
B. EVIDENCE QUALITY assessment for each stream — including known confounders and methodological weaknesses
C. CORRELATED EVIDENCE — identify streams that appear independent but share methodological assumptions (e.g., dietary recall, cohort selection)
D. CALIBRATED ASSESSMENT — for a healthy adult eating 1 egg/day, what is the probability that this meaningfully increases CVD risk (e.g., >5% relative increase)? Give a probability range with conditions (e.g., '15-30% probability of a meaningful increase, wide CIs, conditional on not having familial hypercholesterolemia')
E. WHAT REMAINS UNRESOLVED — what would a decisive study need to look like?

### A. Key Cruxes
1. **The "Hyper-Responder" Threshold:** Does the physiological variability in cholesterol absorption (the "hypo/hyper-responder" phenotype) account for the divergence between population-level null results and individual-level LDL spikes?
2. **The Confounding Matrix:** To what extent is the "egg signal" in Western cohorts merely a proxy for the "Western Dietary Pattern" (e.g., eggs consumed with bacon/refined carbs vs. eggs consumed in isolation)?
3. **The Dose-Response Linearity:** Is there a threshold effect (e.g., 0–3 eggs/week vs. >7/week) that is currently obscured by the broad categorization of "egg consumption" in longitudinal studies?

### B. Evidence Quality Assessment
*   **Prospective Cohorts (Nurses/HPFS):** *Moderate Quality.* High retention, but reliant on Food Frequency Questionnaires (FFQs), which are notoriously prone to recall bias and measurement error.
*   **Chinese Kadoorie Biobank:** *Moderate Quality.* Large N, but limited generalizability to Western populations due to vastly different baseline dietary patterns (e.g., lower saturated fat intake, different cooking methods).
*   **NHANES/MESA (Zhong et al.):** *Moderate-Low Quality.* High risk of residual confounding. While statistically robust, the adjustment for diet quality is often insufficient to isolate the egg variable from the broader lifestyle cluster.
*   **Mechanistic/RCTs:** *High Quality (Internal Validity), Low Quality (External Validity).* Excellent for measuring acute lipid response, but short-term surrogate markers (LDL-C) do not always map linearly to long-term hard clinical endpoints (CVD events).

### C. Correlated Evidence
*   **Shared Methodological Assumptions:** Most large-scale cohorts (Nurses, HPFS, NHANES) rely on **self-reported dietary recall**. This creates a systemic bias where "healthy user bias" (people who eat eggs as part of a "health-conscious" diet) and "unhealthy user bias" (people who eat eggs with processed meats) are baked into the data, potentially canceling out or inflating the signal.
*   **Industry Funding:** A significant portion of the "neutral/beneficial" literature is industry-funded. This correlates with the use of specific study designs (e.g., short-term, small-sample RCTs) that are less likely to detect long-term adverse outcomes.

### D. Calibrated Assessment
For a healthy adult (no familial hypercholesterolemia, no pre-existing CVD) consuming 1 egg/day:
*   **Probability of a >5% relative increase in CVD risk:** **5–15%.**
*   **Conditions:** This estimate assumes a baseline diet that is not otherwise high in saturated/trans fats. If the individual is a "hyper-responder" (approx. 20–25% of the population), the probability shifts toward the higher end of the range. If the individual is a "hypo-responder," the probability is effectively negligible. The wide confidence interval reflects the inability to isolate the egg from the total dietary context.

### E. What Remains Unresolved
A decisive study would require:
1. **Objective Biomarker Tracking:** Moving away from FFQs to metabolomic profiling or controlled feeding trials where the *entire* diet is standardized for a minimum of 12–24 months.
2. **Genomic Stratification:** Pre-screening participants for APOE4 status and other genetic markers of cholesterol metabolism to isolate the "hyper-responder" cohort.
3. **Hard Endpoint Focus:** A large-scale, multi-year RCT with hard CVD endpoints (MI, stroke, mortality) rather than surrogate lipid markers, specifically controlling for the "egg-plus-processed-meat" cluster.
