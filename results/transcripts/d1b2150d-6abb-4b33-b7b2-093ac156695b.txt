BACKGROUND:
The question of whether egg consumption increases cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk has been studied for 50+ years. Dietary guidelines have reversed multiple times. Current expert opinion is split between 'eggs are neutral/beneficial' (American Heart Association revised 2020) and 'eggs increase LDL and CVD risk' (several prospective cohort analyses).

KEY EVIDENCE STREAMS TO CONSIDER:
1. Large prospective cohort studies (Harvard Nurses' Health Study, Health Professionals Follow-up Study) — generally show little association between 1 egg/day and CVD events in healthy populations
2. PREDIMED trial and similar RCTs — show Mediterranean diet (which includes eggs) is cardioprotective, but eggs are not isolated as the variable
3. Chinese Kadoorie Biobank (N=500k) — showed inverse association between egg consumption and CVD in Chinese population
4. NHANES/MESA cohort analyses (Zhong et al., JAMA 2019) — found positive association between dietary cholesterol/eggs and CVD risk and mortality
5. Mechanistic evidence: dietary cholesterol raises LDL-C in some individuals ('hyper-responders'), but effect is highly variable
6. Confounding: egg consumers in Western cohorts often have other unhealthy dietary patterns (high processed meat, low vegetable intake)
7. Industry funding patterns in egg nutrition research and their documented association with null results

Produce a structured epistemic map with the following components:
A. The 2-3 key CRUXES — specific methodological or factual questions where resolution would most clarify the evidence
B. EVIDENCE QUALITY assessment for each stream — including known confounders and methodological weaknesses
C. CORRELATED EVIDENCE — identify streams that appear independent but share methodological assumptions (e.g., dietary recall, cohort selection)
D. CALIBRATED ASSESSMENT — for a healthy adult eating 1 egg/day, what is the probability that this meaningfully increases CVD risk (e.g., >5% relative increase)? Give a probability range with conditions (e.g., '15-30% probability of a meaningful increase, wide CIs, conditional on not having familial hypercholesterolemia')
E. WHAT REMAINS UNRESOLVED — what would a decisive study need to look like?

### A. Key Cruxes
1. **The "Hyper-Responder" Threshold:** We lack a standardized, scalable metric to identify the ~25% of the population who exhibit significant LDL-C increases in response to dietary cholesterol. Without this, population-level averages mask clinically significant individual risks.
2. **The Substitution Effect:** Does the egg replace a refined carbohydrate (neutral/beneficial) or processed meat (harmful)? The evidence fails to isolate the *counterfactual*—what the subject would have eaten instead.

### B. Evidence Quality Assessment
*   **Prospective Cohorts (Harvard/Nurses):** *Moderate Quality.* High N, but relies on Food Frequency Questionnaires (FFQs), which are notoriously prone to recall bias and social desirability bias.
*   **PREDIMED/RCTs:** *High Quality (for diet patterns), Low Quality (for eggs).* Excellent control for overall diet, but eggs are a component of a complex intervention, making isolation of the egg variable impossible.
*   **Chinese Kadoorie Biobank:** *Moderate Quality.* Large scale, but the population's baseline diet (low animal fat, high plant intake) makes it non-generalizable to Western cohorts.
*   **Zhong et al. (JAMA 2019):** *Moderate Quality.* Strong statistical adjustment, but the "healthy user bias" remains a persistent confounder; those who eat eggs in the US often engage in other high-risk behaviors.
*   **Mechanistic Evidence:** *High Quality.* We know cholesterol metabolism is highly variable, but we lack the longitudinal data to link this variability to hard CVD endpoints in a controlled setting.

### C. Correlated Evidence
*   **FFQ Dependency:** Streams 1, 3, and 4 all rely on self-reported dietary recall. They share the same systematic error: the inability to accurately quantify intake over decades.
*   **Healthy User Bias:** Streams 1 and 4 are both susceptible to the "lifestyle cluster" effect, where egg consumption correlates with socioeconomic status and health literacy, creating a shared bias in the direction of the effect.

### D. Calibrated Assessment
For a healthy adult (no familial hypercholesterolemia, no pre-existing CVD) consuming 1 egg/day:
*   **Probability of >5% relative increase in CVD risk:** **5–15%.**
*   **Conditions:** This probability is highly dependent on the *background diet*. If the egg replaces a high-glycemic index breakfast, the risk is likely net-neutral or negative (beneficial). If the egg is added to a high-saturated-fat, high-processed-meat diet, the risk shifts toward the higher end of the range. The wide confidence interval reflects the high inter-individual variability in cholesterol absorption and synthesis.

### E. What Remains Unresolved
A decisive study would require:
1. **N-of-1 Randomized Crossover Trials:** Large-scale, multi-center trials where participants act as their own controls, using objective biomarkers (e.g., LDL-P, ApoB) rather than just LDL-C, and strictly controlling the "substitution" variable (e.g., egg vs. oatmeal vs. bacon).
2. **Genetic Stratification:** Incorporating polygenic risk scores for cholesterol metabolism to identify the "hyper-responders" before the trial begins.
3. **Continuous Glucose/Lipid Monitoring:** Moving away from FFQs toward real-time metabolic tracking to eliminate the "recall" failure point.
