## CALIBRATED EPISTEMIC MAP

### (A) CRUXES

**Crux 1: Confounding vs. causation in Western cohorts**
- Prospective cohorts showing positive associations (NHANES, MESA) are observational. Egg consumption correlates with overall dietary pattern, BMI, exercise, and socioeconomic factors. The causal pathway through eggs specifically vs. the dietary/lifestyle cluster remains unresolved.
- *Why it matters:* If confounding explains the association, the effect size collapses.

**Crux 2: Population heterogeneity (responder status)**
- Dietary cholesterol raises LDL-C substantially in ~25% of people, minimally in others. CVD risk from LDL-C elevation is dose-dependent. The clinical relevance of eggs depends on: (i) what fraction of a population are hyper-responders, (ii) whether LDL-C elevation from eggs translates to CVD events in that subgroup.
- *Why it matters:* A 27% population-level effect could reflect a large effect in 20% of people and null effect in 80%.

**Crux 3: Mechanistic plausibility of inverse associations**
- Chinese Kadoorie shows inverse association. This contradicts the cholesterol-raises-LDL mechanism. Explanations: (i) confounding (healthier people eat more eggs in that cohort), (ii) eggs in that context are part of a different dietary pattern, (iii) the mechanism is wrong or context-dependent.
- *Why it matters:* If the mechanism fails in one large population, the causal model is incomplete.

### (C) CORRELATED EVIDENCE — SHARED METHODOLOGICAL ASSUMPTIONS

**Cluster 1: Western prospective cohorts showing positive association**
- NHANES, MESA, Zhong et al. (JAMA 2019)
- *Shared assumption:* Dietary cholesterol/eggs → LDL-C → CVD events
- *Shared limitation:* Observational; confounding by Western dietary pattern (eggs + processed food + low fiber); reverse causality (people with early CVD reduce eggs)
- *Shared population:* Predominantly white, higher-income, health-conscious (paradoxically, health-conscious people may reduce eggs after CVD diagnosis, biasing results)

**Cluster 2: Null/inverse associations**
- Harvard cohorts, Chinese Kadoorie, Mediterranean diet trials
- *Shared assumption:* Eggs are a minor component of overall diet; context matters
- *Shared limitation:* May underestimate effects in high-consumption subgroups; may reflect survivor bias (people who tolerate eggs well remain in cohort)

**Cluster 3: Mechanistic studies**
- LDL-C response, cholesterol absorption
- *Shared assumption:* LDL-C is the causal pathway
- *Shared limitation:* Surrogate endpoints; ignores other pathways (inflammation, oxidation, microbiota); short-term

### (D) CALIBRATED PROBABILITY RANGE

**Question: Does moderate egg consumption (1 egg/day) increase CVD mortality in the general population?**

**Probability estimate: 25–45%**

**Conditions:**

- **Lower bound (25%):** If confounding in Western cohorts is the primary driver, and the Kadoorie inverse association reflects true heterogeneity, the causal effect is small or null in most populations.
  
- **Upper bound (45%):** If the positive associations in large Western cohorts reflect true causation, and the effect is concentrated in hyper-responders (~25% of population), a 27% relative increase in that subgroup is plausible; population-level effect would be ~7–10% absolute increase in CVD mortality.

- **Most likely range: 30–40%** — The evidence supports a small-to-moderate positive association in Western populations, but causation is not established. The effect is likely heterogeneous by genetics, baseline diet, and population.

**What would move the probability:**
- RCT with CVD events (not surrogates) as endpoint, 5+ years, in a Western population: would clarify causation.
- Mechanistic studies explaining the Kadoorie inverse association: would refine the model.
- Confounding adjustment in prospective cohorts (e.g., propensity score matching on overall diet quality): would test whether association persists.

### (E) WHAT REMAINS UNRESOLVED

1. **Causation vs. confounding:** No RCT with hard CVD endpoints isolates eggs. Observational associations could reflect unmeasured confounding (e.g., overall diet quality, stress, sleep).

2. **Hyper-responder identification:** No validated biomarker or genetic test predicts who will have large LDL-C responses to eggs. Clinical utility is unclear.

3. **Mechanism in inverse-association populations:** Why does Kadoorie show inverse association? Is it confounding, different dietary context, genetic differences, or a false positive?

4. **Dose-response:** Most studies compare "any eggs" vs. "none." The shape of the dose-response curve is unknown. Is there a safe threshold?

5. **Interaction with other factors:** Do eggs interact with fiber intake, exercise, or statin use? The evidence is sparse.

6. **Long-term RCT feasibility:** A definitive RCT (eggs vs. no eggs, 10 years, CVD events) is unlikely to be funded or completed. The evidence base may remain observational.
