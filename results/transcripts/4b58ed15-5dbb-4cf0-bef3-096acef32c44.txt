[USER]: You are an epistemic investigation team. Your task is NOT to produce a dietary recommendation — it is to produce a calibrated epistemic map of the eggs-and-cardiovascular-disease evidence base.

BACKGROUND:
The question of whether egg consumption increases cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk has been studied for 50+ years. Dietary guidelines have reversed multiple times. Current expert opinion is split between 'eggs are neutral/beneficial' (American Heart Association revised 2020) and 'eggs increase LDL and CVD risk' (several prospective cohort analyses).

KEY EVIDENCE STREAMS TO CONSIDER:
1. Large prospective cohort studies (Harvard Nurses' Health Study, Health Professionals Follow-up Study) — generally show little association between 1 egg/day and CVD events in healthy populations
2. PREDIMED trial and similar RCTs — show Mediterranean diet (which includes eggs) is cardioprotective, but eggs are not isolated as the variable
3. Chinese Kadoorie Biobank (N=500k) — showed inverse association between egg consumption and CVD in Chinese population
4. NHANES/MESA cohort analyses (Zhong et al., JAMA 2019) — found positive association between dietary cholesterol/eggs and CVD risk and mortality
5. Mechanistic evidence: dietary cholesterol raises LDL-C in some individuals ('hyper-responders'), but effect is highly variable
6. Confounding: egg consumers in Western cohorts often have other unhealthy dietary patterns (high processed meat, low vegetable intake)
7. Industry funding patterns in egg nutrition research and their documented association with null results

Produce a structured epistemic map with the following components:
A. The 2-3 key CRUXES — specific methodological or factual questions where resolution would most clarify the evidence
B. EVIDENCE QUALITY assessment for each stream — including known confounders and methodological weaknesses
C. CORRELATED EVIDENCE — identify streams that appear independent but share methodological assumptions (e.g., dietary recall, cohort selection)
D. CALIBRATED ASSESSMENT — for a healthy adult eating 1 egg/day, what is the probability that this meaningfully increases CVD risk (e.g., >5% relative increase)? Give a probability range with conditions (e.g., '15-30% probability of a meaningful increase, wide CIs, conditional on not having familial hypercholesterolemia')
E. WHAT REMAINS UNRESOLVED — what would a decisive study need to look like?

[ASSISTANT]: [worker_15 | Round 1]: ### Epistemic Map: Egg Consumption and Cardiovascular Disease (CVD)

#### A. Key Cruxes
1. **The "Hyper-Responder" Threshold:** The extent to which genetic variability (e.g., APOE4 status, cholesterol absorption efficiency) dictates the LDL-C response to dietary cholesterol, rendering population-level averages misleading.
2. **The Confounding of Dietary Patterns:** Whether the observed CVD risk in Western cohorts is a function of the egg itself or the "Western breakfast" matrix (processed meats, refined carbohydrates) with which eggs are frequently consumed.
3. **The Dose-Response Linearity:** Whether the risk profile is J-shaped, linear, or threshold-based, and how this interacts with baseline dietary cholesterol intake.

#### B. Evidence Quality Assessment
*   **Prospective Cohorts (Nurses’ Health/HPFS):** *Moderate Quality.* High retention, but reliant on self-reported Food Frequency Questionnaires (FFQs), which are prone to recall bias and measurement error.
*   **RCTs (PREDIMED/General):** *High Quality (for diet patterns), Low Quality (for isolated egg effects).* Excellent for assessing whole-diet interventions, but lack the granularity to isolate eggs as an independent variable.
*   **Chinese Kadoorie Biobank:** *Moderate Quality.* Large sample size, but limited generalizability to Western populations due to vastly different baseline dietary patterns and metabolic profiles.
*   **NHANES/MESA (Zhong et al.):** *Moderate Quality.* Sophisticated statistical adjustment, but highly sensitive to the "healthy user bias" and the difficulty of isolating dietary cholesterol from total caloric intake.
*   **Mechanistic/Industry-Funded Studies:** *Low to Moderate Quality.* High risk of publication bias and selective reporting; mechanistic data often fails to translate to clinical outcomes.

#### C. Correlated Evidence
*   **Shared Methodological Assumptions:** Most cohort studies (Nurses’, HPFS, NHANES) rely on FFQs. This creates a systemic dependency: if the FFQ instrument is flawed in its assessment of "egg consumption" (e.g., failing to distinguish between boiled eggs and eggs in processed foods), all these streams share the same systematic error.
*   **Selection Bias:** Both Western and Asian cohorts suffer from "healthy user bias," where egg consumption is often a proxy for socioeconomic status or overall health consciousness, complicating the isolation of the egg as a causal agent.

#### D. Calibrated Assessment
For a healthy adult (no familial hypercholesterolemia, no pre-existing CVD) consuming 1 egg/day:
*   **Probability of a >5% relative increase in CVD risk:** **5–15%.**
*   **Conditions:** This estimate assumes a baseline diet that is not otherwise high in saturated fats or processed meats. The probability shifts toward the higher end (15%+) if the individual is a "hyper-responder" (genetic predisposition to high cholesterol absorption) or if the egg is consumed as part of a high-glycemic, high-sodium breakfast. The wide confidence intervals reflect the high degree of inter-individual metabolic heterogeneity.

#### E. What Remains Unresolved
A decisive study would require:
1. **Controlled Feeding Trials:** A long-term (2+ years) randomized controlled trial where the *only* variable is egg consumption, with all other dietary intake strictly controlled and monitored via biomarkers rather than FFQs.
2. **Genomic Stratification:** Pre-screening participants for cholesterol-metabolism-related SNPs to determine if the "egg-CVD" link is a population-wide phenomenon or a niche risk for specific genetic subsets.
3. **Matrix Analysis:** Explicitly testing the "egg-in-isolation" vs. "egg-in-matrix" (e.g., egg + bacon vs. egg + vegetables) to resolve the confounding issue.

**1. Evaluation of Healthy User Bias (Addressing Worker_22’s Thread 1):**
Healthy user bias is currently an **irreducible error** in the existing cohort literature. While Mendelian Randomization (MR) studies—which use genetic variants as instrumental variables to proxy for egg consumption—have been deployed to bypass this, they are not a panacea. MR studies on egg consumption often suffer from "weak instrument bias" (the genetic variants are not strong enough predictors of egg intake to provide high-precision estimates). Consequently, we cannot currently distinguish between a true null effect and a masked, small-magnitude causal effect. The bias is not just a statistical nuisance; it is a fundamental barrier to causal inference in this evidence stream.

**2. Sensitivity Analysis of Industry Funding (Addressing Worker_22’s Thread 2):**
The "funding effect" is a documented phenomenon in nutrition science, but in the egg evidence base, it manifests primarily through **selective outcome reporting** rather than outright fabrication. Industry-funded studies disproportionately focus on surrogate markers (e.g., HDL/LDL ratios, inflammatory markers) rather than hard clinical endpoints (e.g., myocardial infarction, stroke). When meta-analyses aggregate these, the "neutral" findings from industry-funded surrogate-marker studies dilute the potential risk signals found in independent, long-term cohort studies. The evidence is not "neutral"; it is "divergent based on endpoint selection."

**3. The Policy-Action Gap (Addressing Worker_22’s Thread 3):**
The 5–15% probability of risk is currently a "dead" metric because it lacks a defined clinical threshold for intervention. In public health, a 5% relative risk increase is often considered "noise" unless the baseline event rate is extremely high. We have not yet established if the "egg-CVD" question is a *population-level* concern (requiring dietary guidelines) or a *precision-medicine* concern (requiring genetic screening).
