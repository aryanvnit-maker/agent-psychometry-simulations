## NOVEL APPROACH 2: STRATIFY BY METABOLIC PHENOTYPE, NOT POPULATION

Worker_00 correctly identified that Chinese Kadoorie (inverse) and Harvard (null) suggest population effects are conditional. But "population" is too coarse.

**Data source:** Existing RCTs (Zhong et al., others) often report individual-level LDL responses. Meta-analyze those responses, then cross-reference with CVD outcomes in the same cohorts.

**Why this is novel:** It dissolves the population contradiction. Chinese Kadoorie may have fewer hyper-responders (genetic or dietary). Harvard cohorts may have more. Both true. No contradiction.

**Downstream consequence:** Shifts the question from "Are eggs bad?" to "For whom are eggs bad?" This is actionable and testable.

## NOVEL APPROACH 3: TEMPORAL DYNAMICS OF CONFOUNDING

Worker_00 flagged confounding in Western cohorts (egg consumers have other unhealthy patterns). But confounding is not static.

**Approach:** Examine whether confounding *changes over time* within cohorts.

- In Harvard Nurses' Health Study: Do women who eat eggs at baseline differ in other CVD risk factors from women who eat eggs at follow-up?
- If confounding is *stable*, adjustment should work. If confounding *drifts* (e.g., egg consumers become healthier over time, or vice versa), standard adjustment fails.

**Method:** Stratify by baseline confounding burden. Compare:
- Egg consumers with low baseline CVD risk (few other risk factors) vs. high baseline risk
- Do they diverge in CVD outcomes? If yes, confounding is not adequately adjusted.

**Why this is novel:** Most analyses assume confounding is constant. It isn't. Dietary patterns change; health behaviors cluster and uncluster.

**Downstream consequence:** If confounding drifts, we need instrumental variable analysis or Mendelian randomization, not standard adjustment. This changes what evidence is credible.
