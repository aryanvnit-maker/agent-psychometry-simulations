[USER]: You are an epistemic investigation team. Your task is NOT to make an energy policy recommendation — it is to produce a calibrated epistemic map of the comparative safety evidence for nuclear power vs fossil fuels.

BACKGROUND:
The comparative risk of nuclear power is one of the most contested empirical questions in energy policy. Two well-cited positions:

Position A (pro-nuclear): Nuclear power has the lowest deaths per TWh of any major energy source (~0.03/TWh, comparable to wind and solar) when Chernobyl and Fukushima deaths are counted using the same methodology as fossil fuel mortality. Coal kills approximately 800× more people per TWh.

Position B (anti-nuclear): Tail risk analysis invalidates deaths-per-TWh comparisons. A Chernobyl-scale event causes multi-generational harm not captured by near-term mortality counts. Additionally, long-term low-dose radiation effects are systematically undercounted due to the 'linear no-threshold' model controversy.

KEY EVIDENCE STREAMS TO CONSIDER:
1. Deaths per TWh estimates (Our World in Data / Sovacool 2008 / WHO data) — show nuclear comparable to renewables
2. Chernobyl mortality estimates — range from 31 direct deaths (IAEA) to 60,000-985,000 excess cancer deaths (Greenpeace/TORCH reports) to 4,000 (WHO/UNSCEAR consensus)
3. Fukushima mortality: ~1 confirmed radiation death; but ~2,200 deaths from evacuation-related causes
4. Linear no-threshold (LNT) model controversy — is there a safe dose of radiation, or does any exposure carry proportional cancer risk?
5. Coal externalities: PM2.5 mortality is estimated but also model-dependent
6. Institutional sources: IAEA, UNSCEAR, WNA vs Greenpeace, NIRS, Union of Concerned Scientists — systematic directional disagreement
7. Comparison methodology: are we comparing average operations risk or expected-value risk (including tail events with small probability)?

Produce a structured epistemic map:
A. The 2-3 key CRUXES — where does the disagreement actually turn on factual or methodological questions vs value judgments?
B. EVIDENCE QUALITY for each stream — which sources have institutional conflicts of interest, and how does that affect reliability?
C. CORRELATED EVIDENCE — which streams cite the same underlying data source but reach different conclusions, and why?
D. CALIBRATED ASSESSMENT — given the genuine methodological dispute, what is the most defensible probability range for nuclear being safer than coal in expected-value terms? Give conditions.
E. VALUE vs METHODOLOGY — which parts of the disagreement are actually methodological (and potentially resolvable) vs genuine value differences about how to weight tail events?

### A. Key Cruxes
1. **The Temporal Horizon of Harm:** Does "safety" include the multi-generational management of radioactive waste and the potential for long-term land exclusion, or is it strictly limited to immediate/near-term biological mortality?
2. **The LNT Model Validity:** Is the Linear No-Threshold model a scientifically accurate representation of low-dose radiation risk, or is it a precautionary policy construct that artificially inflates the perceived danger of nuclear energy?
3. **The "Normal Accident" vs. "Externality" Framing:** Is nuclear risk an inherent, systemic feature of the technology (Perrow’s Normal Accident Theory), or is it an externalized cost comparable to the atmospheric pollution of fossil fuels?

### B. Evidence Quality
*   **IAEA/UNSCEAR:** High technical rigor regarding direct biological impact, but structurally biased toward the viability of the nuclear industry due to their mandate to promote peaceful nuclear energy.
*   **Greenpeace/TORCH:** High sensitivity to long-term ecological and social disruption, but prone to "worst-case" selection bias and the use of epidemiological models that lack consensus in the broader scientific community.
*   **Our World in Data (OWID):** High transparency in methodology, but relies on the aggregation of existing studies; if the underlying source data (e.g., WHO vs. Greenpeace) is polarized, the "average" is a reflection of the conflict, not a resolution of it.

### C. Correlated Evidence
The Chernobyl mortality estimates are the primary point of divergence. All parties cite the same initial event data, but the variance (31 to 985,000) is driven by:
*   **Attribution:** Whether to count only direct radiation-induced deaths or to include all excess cancer deaths in a massive, heterogeneous population over decades.
*   **Counterfactuals:** The difficulty of isolating radiation-induced cancer from the background noise of lifestyle-related cancers in the post-Soviet population.

### D. Calibrated Assessment
**Probability that nuclear is safer than coal in expected-value terms: 95% (Confidence: High).**
*   **Conditions:** This holds true *only if* the comparison is restricted to mortality per TWh. If the metric is expanded to include "catastrophic societal disruption potential" or "long-term land utility loss," the probability drops to **60-70%**, as the tail risk of nuclear becomes non-negligible compared to the constant, predictable, but massive mortality of coal.

### E. Value vs. Methodology
*   **Methodological (Resolvable):** The LNT model controversy is theoretically resolvable through better longitudinal data on low-dose radiation, though it remains politically fraught. The standardization of "mortality per TWh" is a technical hurdle that could be solved by adopting a unified, transparent accounting framework.
*   **Value-Based (Irresolvable):** The weighting of "tail risk" is a value judgment. A society that prioritizes *predictable, manageable harm* (coal) will view nuclear as unacceptable; a society that prioritizes *minimizing total aggregate mortality* (nuclear) will view the tail risk as a necessary, manageable trade-off. This is not a scientific disagreement; it is a difference in risk appetite and temporal preference.
