BACKGROUND:
The comparative risk of nuclear power is one of the most contested empirical questions in energy policy. Two well-cited positions:

Position A (pro-nuclear): Nuclear power has the lowest deaths per TWh of any major energy source (~0.03/TWh, comparable to wind and solar) when Chernobyl and Fukushima deaths are counted using the same methodology as fossil fuel mortality. Coal kills approximately 800× more people per TWh.

Position B (anti-nuclear): Tail risk analysis invalidates deaths-per-TWh comparisons. A Chernobyl-scale event causes multi-generational harm not captured by near-term mortality counts. Additionally, long-term low-dose radiation effects are systematically undercounted due to the 'linear no-threshold' model controversy.

KEY EVIDENCE STREAMS TO CONSIDER:
1. Deaths per TWh estimates (Our World in Data / Sovacool 2008 / WHO data) — show nuclear comparable to renewables
2. Chernobyl mortality estimates — range from 31 direct deaths (IAEA) to 60,000-985,000 excess cancer deaths (Greenpeace/TORCH reports) to 4,000 (WHO/UNSCEAR consensus)
3. Fukushima mortality: ~1 confirmed radiation death; but ~2,200 deaths from evacuation-related causes
4. Linear no-threshold (LNT) model controversy — is there a safe dose of radiation, or does any exposure carry proportional cancer risk?
5. Coal externalities: PM2.5 mortality is estimated but also model-dependent
6. Institutional sources: IAEA, UNSCEAR, WNA vs Greenpeace, NIRS, Union of Concerned Scientists — systematic directional disagreement
7. Comparison methodology: are we comparing average operations risk or expected-value risk (including tail events with small probability)?

Produce a structured epistemic map:
A. The 2-3 key CRUXES — where does the disagreement actually turn on factual or methodological questions vs value judgments?
B. EVIDENCE QUALITY for each stream — which sources have institutional conflicts of interest, and how does that affect reliability?
C. CORRELATED EVIDENCE — which streams cite the same underlying data source but reach different conclusions, and why?

E. VALUE vs METHODOLOGY — which parts of the disagreement are actually methodological (and potentially resolvable) vs genuine value differences about how to weight tail events?

### A. Key Cruxes
1. **The Temporal/Spatial Boundary Problem:** Does the "deaths per TWh" metric capture the full cost of a catastrophic event? The disagreement turns on whether we treat nuclear risk as a *frequentist average* (operational safety) or a *Bayesian tail-risk* (catastrophic potential).
2. **The LNT Model Validity:** Is the Linear No-Threshold model a conservative safety standard or a scientific error? This is a factual dispute regarding biological response to low-dose radiation that dictates the entire mortality projection for nuclear accidents.
3. **The Counterfactual Baseline:** How do we measure the "cost" of fossil fuel mortality? Disagreement exists on whether to include only direct respiratory/cardiovascular deaths (PM2.5) or to include the systemic mortality associated with climate-driven displacement and extreme weather.

### B. Evidence Quality & Institutional Bias
*   **IAEA/UNSCEAR:** High technical rigor, but structurally incentivized to minimize the perceived risk of nuclear energy to maintain industry viability.
*   **Greenpeace/NIRS:** High advocacy orientation; they utilize "worst-case" epidemiological modeling (e.g., the TORCH report) which often ignores the confounding variables of lifestyle and socio-economic collapse in post-Soviet regions.
*   **Our World in Data (OWID):** Aggregates peer-reviewed meta-analyses. High reliability for operational data, but struggles to integrate "tail risk" because such risks are inherently non-frequentist.

### C. Correlated Evidence
The Chernobyl mortality estimates (31 vs. 4,000 vs. 60,000+) all draw from the same initial exposure data. The divergence is entirely driven by:
1. **Attribution:** Whether to count deaths from non-radiation causes (e.g., alcoholism, poverty) in the exclusion zone as "nuclear deaths."
2. **Extrapolation:** Whether to apply LNT to the entire population of Europe (leading to the 60k+ figures) or restrict it to the high-exposure cohorts.

### D. Calibrated Assessment
**Probability Range:** There is a **90% probability** that nuclear power is safer than coal in expected-value terms, *provided* the comparison is restricted to operational and acute-event mortality. 
**Conditions:** This probability drops to **60-70%** if one accepts the LNT model as absolute truth and applies it to the entire global population exposed to low-level radiation from all historical accidents. The "safer" conclusion is robust unless one assigns a high weight to "catastrophic tail risk" (the possibility of a multi-generational, civilization-altering event).

### E. Value vs. Methodology
*   **Methodological:** The LNT controversy and the PM2.5 attribution models are empirical questions. They are theoretically resolvable through better longitudinal epidemiological studies and improved atmospheric modeling.
*   **Value-Based:** The weighting of "tail risk" is a value judgment. A society that prioritizes *resilience* (avoiding irreversible, large-scale damage) will view nuclear differently than a society that prioritizes *efficiency* (minimizing immediate, predictable mortality). This is not a scientific dispute; it is a political one.
