BACKGROUND:
The comparative risk of nuclear power is one of the most contested empirical questions in energy policy. Two well-cited positions:

Position A (pro-nuclear): Nuclear power has the lowest deaths per TWh of any major energy source (~0.03/TWh, comparable to wind and solar) when Chernobyl and Fukushima deaths are counted using the same methodology as fossil fuel mortality. Coal kills approximately 800× more people per TWh.

Position B (anti-nuclear): Tail risk analysis invalidates deaths-per-TWh comparisons. A Chernobyl-scale event causes multi-generational harm not captured by near-term mortality counts. Additionally, long-term low-dose radiation effects are systematically undercounted due to the 'linear no-threshold' model controversy.

KEY EVIDENCE STREAMS TO CONSIDER:
1. Deaths per TWh estimates (Our World in Data / Sovacool 2008 / WHO data) — show nuclear comparable to renewables
2. Chernobyl mortality estimates — range from 31 direct deaths (IAEA) to 60,000-985,000 excess cancer deaths (Greenpeace/TORCH reports) to 4,000 (WHO/UNSCEAR consensus)
3. Fukushima mortality: ~1 confirmed radiation death; but ~2,200 deaths from evacuation-related causes
4. Linear no-threshold (LNT) model controversy — is there a safe dose of radiation, or does any exposure carry proportional cancer risk?
5. Coal externalities: PM2.5 mortality is estimated but also model-dependent
6. Institutional sources: IAEA, UNSCEAR, WNA vs Greenpeace, NIRS, Union of Concerned Scientists — systematic directional disagreement
7. Comparison methodology: are we comparing average operations risk or expected-value risk (including tail events with small probability)?

Produce a structured epistemic map:
A. The 2-3 key CRUXES — where does the disagreement actually turn on factual or methodological questions vs value judgments?
B. EVIDENCE QUALITY for each stream — which sources have institutional conflicts of interest, and how does that affect reliability?

### 1. The "Expected Value" vs. "Precautionary" Divide
The divide is not merely about risk; it is about **ontology**. 
*   **Actuarial (Pro-Nuclear):** Defines safety as the minimization of *aggregate, observable mortality*. It treats the world as a system of predictable, repeatable events.
*   **Precautionary (Anti-Nuclear):** Defines safety as the minimization of *irreversible, systemic disruption*. It treats the world as a complex system where "tail events" (Black Swans) can trigger cascading failures that exceed the capacity of the system to recover. 
*   **Synthesis:** You cannot reconcile these. One is a calculation of *frequency*; the other is a calculation of *resilience*.

### 2. The Epistemic Mechanism of Institutional Conflict
The institutions cannot converge because they operate on different **"Units of Harm"**:
*   **IAEA/UNSCEAR:** Use **Individual Mortality** as the unit. This allows them to "dilute" the impact of a disaster across a large population, making the per-capita risk appear statistically negligible.
*   **NGOs (Greenpeace/NIRS):** Use **Societal/Ecological Integrity** as the unit. They count the *displacement* of populations, the *loss of land utility*, and the *intergenerational burden* of waste as "harm." 
*   **The Mechanism:** The IAEA ignores the "cost of displacement" as a non-radiological externality; the NGOs treat it as the primary consequence of the technology. They are literally measuring different things.

### 3. Final Synthesis: Empirical vs. Normative
*   **Empirical (Resolvable):** We can reach consensus on the *direct* mortality of radiation exposure if we agree on the LNT model's parameters. We can reach consensus on PM2.5 mortality if we standardize air quality modeling. These are technical hurdles.
*   **Normative (Irresolvable):** We cannot reach consensus on whether a 1-in-10,000-year event that renders a city uninhabitable is "worth it" for 10,000 years of carbon-free energy. This is a value judgment regarding **intergenerational equity** and **risk tolerance**.
