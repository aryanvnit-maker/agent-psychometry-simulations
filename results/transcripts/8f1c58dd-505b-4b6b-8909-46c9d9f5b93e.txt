EVIDENCE BASE (eggs and cardiovascular disease):
1. Large prospective cohort studies (Harvard Nurses' Health Study, Health Professionals Follow-up Study) — little association between 1 egg/day and CVD events in healthy populations
2. PREDIMED and similar RCTs — Mediterranean diet (includes eggs) is cardioprotective, but eggs are not isolated as the variable
3. Chinese Kadoorie Biobank (N=500k) — inverse association in Chinese population
4. NHANES/MESA cohort analyses (Zhong et al., JAMA 2019) — positive association between dietary cholesterol/eggs and CVD risk
5. Mechanistic: dietary cholesterol raises LDL-C in some 'hyper-responders', highly variable
6. Confounding: egg consumers in Western cohorts often have other unhealthy dietary patterns
7. Industry funding patterns and their documented association with null results

Produce a calibrated epistemic map: (A) 2-3 cruxes, (B) evidence-quality assessment per stream, (C) correlated evidence — streams that appear independent but share methodological assumptions, (D) a calibrated probability range with conditions, (E) what remains unresolved.

2. **Confounding vs. Causality:** Whether the positive associations in Western cohorts (Zhong et al.) are artifacts of the "unhealthy user bias" (where egg consumption correlates with high-saturated-fat/processed-meat diets) or represent an independent causal pathway.

**B. Evidence-Quality Assessment**
*   **Prospective Cohorts (Harvard/Nurses/HPFS):** High quality for observational data; large sample sizes and long follow-up. Reliability is high for the "no association" claim in healthy populations.
*   **NHANES/MESA (Zhong et al.):** High quality for data collection, but moderate for causal inference due to reliance on self-reported dietary data and potential residual confounding.
*   **Chinese Kadoorie Biobank:** High quality for large-scale observational data; however, limited generalizability to Western populations due to distinct dietary and lifestyle contexts.
*   **Mechanistic Studies:** Moderate quality; high internal validity regarding cholesterol metabolism, but low external validity regarding clinical CVD outcomes.
*   **Industry-Funded Studies:** Low quality due to systematic bias; these are excluded from the primary weight of evidence.

**C. Correlated Evidence**
*   **Methodological Assumption:** The Harvard cohorts and NHANES/MESA share a reliance on Food Frequency Questionnaires (FFQs). This is a shared vulnerability; if FFQs systematically misclassify egg intake or associated dietary patterns, the "independent replication" is weakened by a shared systematic error.
*   **Dietary Matrix:** The PREDIMED trial and the Chinese Kadoorie Biobank are correlated by the fact that eggs are consumed within a broader, often cardioprotective, dietary pattern. They do not isolate the egg, meaning their "protective" signals may be attributed to the matrix rather than the egg itself.

**D. Calibrated Probability Range**
*   **Probability:** 75–85% confidence that 1 egg/day does not increase CVD risk in the general, healthy population.
*   **Conditions:** This probability holds provided the individual does not have pre-existing metabolic syndrome or hyper-responsiveness to dietary cholesterol, and provided the egg is not consumed as part of a high-saturated-fat/processed-meat dietary pattern.

**E. Unresolved Threads**
*   **The "Hyper-Responder" Identification:** There is no consensus on a clinical screening tool to identify individuals who should limit egg intake based on their specific LDL-C response.
*   **The "Matrix Effect":** We lack granular data on how the *preparation* of the egg (e.g., fried in butter vs. poached) and the *accompanying foods* (e.g., bacon vs. vegetables) modulate the CVD risk signal.
*   **Causal Mechanism:** The discrepancy between the mechanistic evidence (dietary cholesterol raises LDL-C) and the clinical outcome evidence (no CVD increase) remains unresolved; the "LDL-C as a proxy" model may be insufficient to explain the clinical reality of egg consumption.
